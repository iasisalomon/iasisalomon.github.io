## Abstract

We can know the scientific, philosophical and humanist thought of Albert Einstein (1879–1955) through his legacy and the chronicles of his life. As a university student, it is a humbling challenge to interpret the iconic genius of the twentieth century by projecting his thinking on how human beings can change the way they live and act in the world.

To find a synthesis between two generations living on the border between two centuries, I chose to set them against each other through readings and revisionist conversations with my father. I hope this way of working helps to interpret the contemporary influence received from Einstein's worldview.

My only personal contact and conversation with a Nobel laureate (HUJI) was with Prof. Dr. Robert Aumann (Game Theory, 2005), invited by Grupo Financiero Rosental to give a lecture at the Rosario Board of Trade, Argentina, in November 2009.

In 1934, in his book "The World as I See It", Einstein laid down the foundations of his thinking. The text is complemented by a series of widely known sayings of deep ethical content and reflective definitions that suit the work of this competition.

During his visit to Argentina, the brilliant physicist wrote some articles for the newspaper La Prensa; in one of them, Einstein anticipated his wish to pass on his view of the world as a testimony of his thoughts. By the time this book appeared, Einstein (Nobel Prize in Physics, 1921) had already extended his influence in Argentina with a series of lectures between March 24 and April 24, 1925.

He arrived in Buenos Aires at the age of 46 and stayed with the influential Wasserman family. No eyewitnesses of those momentous events remain, only photographs and newspaper articles that bear witness to how his ideals were spread.

His lectures in Argentina and around the world left their mark on politicians, rulers, deans, scientists, intellectuals and students. His ideas influenced international politics, philosophy, the arts and the sciences. In the sciences in particular, in the fields of electronics, energy, lasers, unification theories, particle accelerators and quantum entanglement: all of them lines of research that make up challenging chapters of knowledge in the twenty-first century.

## His written thought

Facing the rise of Nazism, Einstein left Germany for the United States in 1932. Two years later his book on his view of the world at that time was published. As a philosopher, Einstein is understood in the modern sense as a logical positivist, a man interested in the foundations of knowledge. He gave clarity to his fundamental scientific concepts, considering science a creation of the mind, a free human invention. The most characteristic features of his work are originality and obstinacy, together with the ability to walk a solitary path for years, based on thought and interpretation.

In his book on his view of the world, and despite all human effort, Einstein laments our short passage through life towards death without learning, in that span, our essential purpose. He reflects on the absurdity of searching for the meaning and object of life. He states that we are bound to people and groups, known or unknown, living and dead, on whom our happiness, work and destiny depend. He sets out a reflective everyday human life in two spaces of the self: one inner and our own, and one outer, received from others, for whom we must strive. He says that existence itself consumes a disproportionate share of the work of others. Einstein does not justify class differences, least of all when they are born of force. He prefers and recommends a life of searching, simple and modest. He disbelieves in human freedom in philosophical terms, since it is subject to outer and inner pressures that change it from time to time. He singles out as revealing the thought of Schopenhauer, which guided him through the adversities of his life and his learning of tolerance, and quotes him: "A man can do as he wills, but not will as he wills".
Einstein says that understanding softens seriousness and the weight of responsibilities, and he gives humor a significant place, which he himself displayed in witty public replies and photographs of himself. He states that it is ideals that set the direction of our efforts and judgments, and claims as his own supreme ideals beauty, goodness and truth. He holds that human life is empty if it is not shared with the community, without the objective world, without the unreachable dream of perfection in the arts and sciences. Finally, he scorns excessive possessions, the vanity of public success, and luxury.

Curiously, in his book Einstein sets out a series of principles he deeply believes in, and uses terms such as "social justice", "social responsibility" and "pan-Europeanism", realities ahead of his time. The sage sees his own life as that of a "lone traveler", without clinging to a country, a home, friends or his immediate family.

Politically, Einstein supported democracy and the individual, and opposed idolatry and idols. He criticizes himself in this respect, for the admiration the world grants him on the strength of the few ideas he managed to connect in his ceaseless search for knowledge.
He is aware, and says so, that a society achieves its goals when someone chosen by that society thinks, leads and takes on responsibilities without the rule of force or coercion, and he adds that autocracies degenerate, promoting roguish men of low morals who turn their peoples into "herds", as was happening at the time in Italy and Russia.
He saw in Europe in general a lack of stability in its governments; by contrast, he believed in the democracy of the United States, in the continuity of its presidential system, with real power and a limited term of office. Einstein adds that the human spectacle is not the "political state" that creates "herds" of feelings and thoughts, but the individual in their own free will, with a curious, creative personality in its eternal search for the sublime.
He also warns that the worst of "herd life" is the military system, with its displays of force and organization, which he "abhors" and calls a "spectacle for the brainless".
He finds exacerbated patriotism, hero worship and senseless violence abominable. Einstein uses unusually harsh words when he speaks of the military system and of war, which he calls a "repugnant absurdity", with "vile and despicable" acts; he even writes in the first person… "How passionately I hate it!" He then goes further, saying he would rather suffer a painful death than be part of something so abominable.

Einstein believes in the individual and in humankind, and then strongly criticizes the political and economic interests that act through schools and the press, swaying people's common sense and promoting political and military adventures. Other influential contemporary thinkers, such as Sartre, shared his reflections on being, freedom and nonviolence.
In a famous sentence, Sartre summed it up: "Violence, in whatever form it appears, is a failure". In his committed, global outlook, Einstein especially admired Gandhi as a pacifist and thinker, as personal letters and photographs of their meetings record. In letters dated 1932, Einstein also discussed violence with Freud, whom he consulted about wars between nations and civil wars, along with the drives of hatred inherent in man and the social issues that give rise to them.

In another part of his book, about his love for science in relation to man, he describes the most beautiful and intimate experience of individuals as the mysterious, a fundamental emotion that is the creative source of the arts and sciences. He considers dead the person who can no longer admire, marvel and feel awe at the endless mystery that surrounds us.

As for religions, Einstein believes they are founded on mystery and fear, and on the utopian certainty that something exists that we will never reach with even the deepest reason. He calls himself religious, but only in his belief in the unreachable nature of reason and beauty. He does not believe in a God of rewards and punishments with a will like human thought, nor can he, or does he want to, imagine anyone surviving their own death. With some irony, he advises us to leave such ideas to the weak souls who, out of fear or selfishness, take comfort in them.

Einstein concludes by feeling satisfied with his awe at the eternal mystery of life, and with having glimpsed a small part of the real world in which nature reveals itself.

## Einstein's legacy in the twenty-first century

Einstein is considered the father of the atomic bomb, since the modern history of atomic energy begins with his famous relation between mass and energy. Yet Einstein, who despised violence and war, was caught by the history of his century and by his own principles in the face of German scientific and military progress.
Recall that Germany invaded Poland in September 1939, starting the Second World War.
The inner and outer worlds he had described in his book five years earlier came into conflict; he began to feel tormented by the responsibility of his leadership, which forced him to make a paradoxical decision.
The speed of the events of war drove him to send his famous letter of August 2, 1939 to President Roosevelt, which reads: "In the course of the last four months it has been made probable - through the work of Joliot in France as well as Fermi and Szilard in the United States - that a nuclear chain reaction could be started in a large mass of uranium" (...) "This new phenomenon could lead to the construction of bombs, and it is conceivable - I think inevitable - that extremely powerful bombs of a new type may be constructed".
He then advises "speeding up the experimental work, which is at present being carried on within the limits of the budgets of university laboratories, by providing funds. If such funds be needed, through contacts with private persons who are willing to make contributions for this cause, and perhaps also by obtaining the cooperation of industrial laboratories which have the necessary equipment".

In the twenty-first century, the most outstanding part of Einstein's ideological legacy is the understanding that the military use of nuclear weapons is incompatible with life on the planet, brilliantly summed up in his words: "I do not know what World War III will be like, but World War IV will be fought with sticks and stones".

The twentieth century left a toll of some 100 million dead in two world wars and several regional wars, along with the horror of the devastating effects of the nuclear holocaust in Japan.
The planet, the fragile ship we live on, which feeds and carries us all through the universe, is attacked by ourselves on a scale that upsets its sustainable balance: the environment, overpopulation, natural resources, refugees, water, food, wars, energy, global warming, illiteracy, technology, the concentration of wealth, and more.
Since Einstein was born, the population has grown from 1.7 billion to 7.5 billion; the pressure on the planet's environment and resources has grown on that scale, to dangerous limits. The political and military structure of the bipolar Cold War gave way in the twenty-first century to a multipolar world, now with a strong presence of China disputing global leadership, and outer space, with the United States.
Today's global landscape shows regional political conflicts of many kinds: strategic, religious, terrorist, drug trafficking, energy and warlike, in an unstable balance. The wealth of the global economy is increasingly concentrated among those who hold knowledge and technology, compared with the countries that own natural resources. None of this reflects the vision of happiness suggested by the bonds of affinity between beings and societies, between the living and the dead, nor the modesty, simplicity and responsibility of leaders that Einstein's vision calls for in his book.

With his Special Theory of Relativity, Einstein brought changes to epistemology and psychology regarding the objectivity or subjectivity of points of view, prompting new ways of thinking about the mental processes involved in perception.
These ideas also had a strong impact on art: on Cubism, with its multiple points of view; on Expressionism, with its subjective vision of reality through distorted images; on Fauvism, which eliminated perspective; on Surrealism, which combined reality and unreality. Clearly the imprint of Einstein's thought created new visions in every field of knowledge; yet he made clear in his writings that inspiring free will has its own ethical limits.
In this century, advances in genetics have let us go deeper into the essence of human origins and our evolutionary code. Einstein considered it absurd to look for the meaning or object of human life, whose mere presence is its meaning, and warned that it is ideals that shape the evolutionary course of our existence, highlighting intangible human values as the essential drivers of that evolution.

Einstein defines himself as a democrat, with rulers who take on their responsibilities; he promotes respect for individuals while detesting idols, and anticipates pan-Europeanism in a world that was changing fast as the century went on. His proposal would later take shape with the creation of the United Nations in 1945, the European Union in 1993, and the rise of blocs and groups of countries that discuss joint strategies.

Indeed, the twenty-first century is transformed, creating new scenarios out of the global political and economic changes that followed the two world wars: the United States opens up the Chinese market. Glasnost arrives, the Berlin Wall and the USSR fall. Pan-Europeanism begins with the creation of the EU. Africa bleeds in civil wars, South Africa ends Apartheid, and Latin America puts an end to its military dictatorships and recovers democratic government.

Curiously, Einstein, who defines himself as a "lone traveler", counts among the values he wants to promote other political concepts that were ahead of his time and reach into this century: "social justice" and "social responsibility". Social justice reaches the present through different political conceptions: for liberalism, social justice is tied to creating opportunities and to private initiative, while socialism and other left-wing proposals focus on state intervention for a better social response. Social responsibility, as Einstein advocates it, refers to the commitment a government has towards others or towards the societies it belongs to; these responsibilities are expressed in ethical international agreements such as UNESCO's declarations on bioethics or the UN's Universal Declaration of Human Rights. On a smaller scale, the concept has today been projected into what is called Corporate Social Responsibility: corporate actions that extend the implications of an institution's conduct into the community where it operates.

Einstein abhorred and warned against autocratic systems, because they degenerate as they cling to power. Such systems still spread and persist today in Asia, the Middle East and Latin America, creating high-risk regional political and military conflicts when they manage to reach the international stage, especially when they touch on the development of nuclear weapons or on dealings around them that lack transparency.

Einstein believed in the democratic system of the United States, which he recognized for its importance, permanence and example. Yet the United States, with a stable democratic system and alternation of parties in power, enters this century with its government's leadership heavily conditioned by the economic interests of its dominant sectors. The consequences show in its conflicts over migration and social welfare, terrorism on its own soil, the involvement of drug trafficking in its banking system, and the global financial imbalances caused by its political practices and economic interests.

Einstein praised the liberality of the United States, which allows ideas to develop, but noted that, unlike Europe, it leaves the individual without social protection against adversity, and with social and ethnic conflicts that remain unresolved today.

In his writings, Einstein contrasts individual development and cooperation, as the creative space of a nation, with societies led as "herds", whose thoughts and feelings are dulled by that practice through religious fanaticism, inflamed masses and dependent populism. These kinds of political leadership, which Einstein rejected, have continued and spread to the present day, showing the tragedy of how they lead their peoples into crisis and hardship.

Einstein says "I have a high opinion of the human race", accusing the political and economic interests that act through education and the press of "breaking people's common sense". He was denouncing a practice that has since been perfected and that, paradoxically, is shaped by his own discoveries. In electronics, he influenced semiconductors, GPS and spintronics; in energy, solar panels, nuclear energy and antimatter; laser technologies; theories of electromagnetic unification; particle accelerators; and proposals based on quantum entanglement for secure communication, teleportation and quantum computers.

With the evolution of communications and computing, the twenty-first century brought the global reach of mass media, the centralization of information, and mass behavior in the form of new "herds" held captive by the digital age. People have been connected globally, but the individual has been isolated, leaving man connected yet alone. All these inventions, born of the technical evolution of his brilliant theories, have produced social behaviors that Einstein criticized, with consequences he himself could neither imagine nor foresee.

## Final message and conclusion

Einstein ends his book with a message essential to the identity of the human condition when he says that the endless experience is the "mysterious", the cradle of all art and science in their eternal renewal. He believes in the originating essence of admiration and of the awe of marveling at the unknown, which he identifies as the spark that starts a new search. After his death, his own brain became the spark for study by the neuroscience community: it was dissected into thousands of pieces to be analyzed by dozens of researchers, who concluded that his exceptional ability lay in a greater density of neurons and of the glial cells surrounding them, with a more folded morphology, in areas of sensory perception and the prefrontal cortex, forming atypical anatomical organizations between the motor and the conceptual. Einstein stated clearly that "mystery" and "fear" are the source of the religious beliefs that will always keep man awake at night, and summed them up in these exact words, setting out a definitive axiom: "The certainty that there is something we can never reach".

Einstein taught the world plainly that his religiosity consisted in the powerlessness of never reaching the deepest reason or the most radiant beauty. He showed that he did not believe in a God who thinks of rewards and punishments in the manner of human will; he could not and would not imagine anyone surviving their physical death, and he left that mysticism to the weakest, who out of fear or selfishness may find comfort in such ideas.

Einstein was content with the mystery of the eternity of life, fully aware of the effort it took to grasp at least a very small part of the reason of nature. For his work, he did not reproach himself for where his discoveries and future influence would lead.

In 1955 Bertrand Russell drafted a document known as the Russell-Einstein Manifesto, on the dangers of nuclear proliferation; Einstein signed it shortly before his death on April 18 of that year. The clarity, modesty and grandeur of the reach of his thought are undoubtedly a legacy to humanity and for all time.

This century receives his reflections in light of the decisions made in the last one, which bear on the possible and probable futures of humanity, but whose final fate is part of that "essential mystery" of things. The sage invited us to look at them every day with wonder, taking part, as he did, in some tiny piece of the natural order around us and of the world that carries us through a colossal, astonishing, infinite universe of unknown destiny. To close, in a poetic way, I use a fragment by García Lorca that sums up the genius's outlook: "And Time has fallen asleep forever in its tower. All the clocks deceive us".
